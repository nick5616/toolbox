# Ten basic drum beats, explained

Almost every song you know is built on a small handful of drum patterns. Learn these ten and you can recognise the engine underneath most pop, rock, funk and electronic music. Each one is written in counts, where "1 and 2 and 3 and 4 and" is one bar.

## The foundation

**1. Eighth-note rock beat.** The hi-hat plays on every count and every "and". The kick lands on one and three, and the snare lands on two and four. This is the pattern most people picture when they think of a drummer, and it carries an enormous amount of music.

**2. Quarter-note pulse.** The same kick and snare, but the hi-hat plays only on the counts. It sounds calmer and leaves more space for a singer. Ballads and slower songs lean on it.

**3. Four on the floor.** The kick drum plays on every count, with a clap or snare on two and four and an open hi-hat on each "and". It is the backbone of dance music, and it makes people move without thinking about it.

## Adding movement

**4. Kick variation.** Take the rock beat and add one extra kick just before the third count, on the "and" of two. That single note turns a plain pattern into a groove, and it is the easiest way to make a beat feel alive.

**5. Half-time.** The snare moves to count three only, so the beat feels half as fast while the tempo stays the same. Heavy and slow-sounding, it is common in hip-hop and in the big choruses of rock songs.

**6. Sixteenth-note hi-hats.** The hi-hat plays four notes for every count, so the sound shimmers. Funk and modern pop use it to add energy without changing the kick and snare underneath.

## Feel and swing

**7. The shuffle.** Every count is split into three notes instead of two, with the middle one left silent. The result bounces, and it is the heart of blues and early rock and roll.

**8. Bossa nova.** A gentle pattern with the kick on one and the "and" of two, a cross-stick on the snare, and soft hi-hat. It is relaxed, even and quietly complex.

**9. The backbeat with ghost notes.** Very quiet snare notes are placed between the loud ones on two and four. You barely hear them, but you would notice if they were gone. Soul and funk drummers use them all the time.

**10. The fill.** A fill is a short burst, usually in the last bar of a four-bar phrase, that tells everyone the section is about to change. A simple fill is four snare hits on the counts of the final bar, then a crash cymbal on the next one.

## How to practise

Start slowly, around sixty beats per minute, with a metronome. Play each pattern for one full minute without speeding up. When it feels easy, raise the tempo by five beats per minute. Count out loud, because counting is what keeps your hands and feet in time with each other.

Listen for these patterns in your favourite songs. Once you can name the beat, you can start to hear why the song feels the way it does.
